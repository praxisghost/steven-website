Things I Want to Learn More About

Subjects I haven't studied deeply yet but genuinely want to.

This page is a record of intellectual curiosity rather than formal study plans. The items here are areas I find compelling and want to understand better — not credentials I'm working toward.

Physics

Physics is the subject I most regret not studying more seriously in school. I've read popular accounts of quantum mechanics, relativity, and thermodynamics and found them fascinating — but I'm aware that popular accounts only go so far. I want to understand the mathematics that underlies those ideas, not just the metaphors used to explain them to general audiences.

Classical mechanics would be the right starting point: Newtonian physics, then the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations. From there, electromagnetism, and eventually the deeper theories. It's a long project, but one I find genuinely motivating.

Chemistry

Chemistry connects to almost everything I care about: biology, materials, energy, the environment. I have a basic understanding of atomic structure, bonding, and chemical reactions from school, but I never went far enough to find chemistry truly satisfying. I want to understand organic chemistry well enough to follow biochemical reasoning, and physical chemistry well enough to understand thermodynamics from a molecular perspective.

There's also something appealing about chemistry as a practical discipline. The lab work is real and tangible in a way that a lot of abstract learning isn't.

Biology

I'm drawn to biology at the intersection of evolution, ecology, and behavior. How do organisms adapt? How do ecosystems function? What are the mechanisms of heredity and gene expression? These are questions I find deeply interesting.

I'm particularly curious about herpetology — the biology of amphibians and reptiles — and about aquatic ecosystems. My interest in frogs is more than casual; I'd like to understand their biology seriously. I'm also interested in conservation biology and the science of what it actually takes to preserve biodiversity.

Mathematics

My mathematical education stopped at the calculus level and I've always felt the gap. I want to understand linear algebra properly — it underlies too much of modern computing and data science to remain vague about. I'd also like to get comfortable with probability and statistics at a formal level, not just an intuitive one.

Beyond the immediately useful, I'm curious about discrete mathematics and combinatorics, number theory, and the philosophy of mathematics. The question of what mathematical truth is and why mathematics describes the physical world so well is one of the most interesting puzzles I know of.

Psychology

Psychology intersects with almost everything I do: teaching, language learning, cross-cultural communication, public health. I have an applied interest in learning science — how memory works, what makes instruction effective, what conditions support motivation and habit formation. A lot of what I know here is practical rather than formally grounded, and I'd like to change that.

I'm also interested in social and cultural psychology: how context shapes perception, how groups form and maintain identity, how beliefs are transmitted and changed. These questions matter for teaching, for policy, and just for understanding people.

Sustainable Energy

The transition away from fossil fuels is one of the defining challenges of my lifetime and I want to understand it better than I currently do. That means both the technology side — solar, wind, grid storage, hydrogen, nuclear — and the policy, economic, and social dimensions of how that transition happens in practice.

I'm interested in the systems-level view: how does a modern electrical grid actually work, and what does it take to make one that runs on intermittent sources? What are the real trade-offs between different clean energy technologies? I don't have a strong technical background in this area yet, but I find the questions compelling enough to want to build one.
