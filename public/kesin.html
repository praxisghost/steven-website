1. Language Overview

Kesin is a constructed auxiliary language of my own design. It draws systematically from three typologically distinct linguistic families — German, Turkish, and Latin — producing a language that is simultaneously agglutinative (like Turkish), case-rich (like Latin), and phonologically familiar to speakers of Central European languages (like German).

The full feature set:

  • 8 noun cases
  • 3 grammatical genders
  • 20 tense-aspect combinations
  • 3 moods (Indicative, Conjunctive, Optative) plus the Imperative
  • 3 voices (Active, Neutral/Middle, Passive)
  • Fully agglutinative verb morphology with a six-slot structure
  • Vowel harmony in selected suffixes
  • Largely free word order — meaning is carried by case marking
Source languagePrimary contributions
GermanCore vocabulary, umlaut vowels (ä, ö, ü), V2 word-order option, conditional suffix -würd-
TurkishAgglutinative suffix system, vowel harmony, negation (-me-), question marker (-mi-), progressive (-iyor-), future (-ecek-), evidential (-miş-)
LatinEight-case noun system, three grammatical genders, free word order, occasional lexical roots

2. Design Philosophy

  • Maximum regularity. Every verb without exception follows the same six-slot assembly. Learn the system once and you can conjugate any verb you ever encounter.
  • Maximum expressiveness. The 20 tense-aspect combinations cover every temporal and aspectual distinction commonly found across human languages.
  • Word-order freedom through case marking. Because every noun argument is unambiguously case-marked, constituents can appear in nearly any order.
  • Familiar lexicon. Vocabulary leans on German roots, with the Turkish-derived agglutinative machinery and Latin-style case framework.

3. Phonology & Pronunciation

Vowel Inventory

Kesin has eight vowels, divided into back vowels (a, o, u) and front vowels (e, i, ä, ö, ü). This distinction governs vowel harmony in suffixes.

VowelIPATypeAs in…
a[a]backGerman Vater
e[ɛ]frontGerman Bett

o[o]backGerman Boot
u[u]backGerman gut

ö[ø]frontGerman schön
ü[y]frontGerman über

Consonants

Consonants broadly follow German orthographic conventions, with two additional letters from Turkish:

  • ğ — a soft, near-silent lengthening of the preceding vowel (as in Turkish dağ)
  • ş — /ʃ/, the sound in English "shoe" (as in Turkish şehir)

All other consonants are pronounced as in standard German.

Vowel Harmony

Suffix vowels adapt to the stem's final vowel:

  • Back-vowel stems (last vowel a, o, u) → suffixes use back vowels
  • Front-vowel stems (last vowel e, i, ä, ö, ü) → suffixes use front vowels

Turkish-origin tense markers (-iyor-, -ecek-, -miş-, -gel-) are invariable — they do not harmonise. Only the negation marker -me- harmonises: -me- for front-vowel stems and -ma- for back-vowel stems. For consistency, this guide uses -me- in all examples; -ma- is an accepted dialectal variant.

Double-Vowel Reduction (DVR)

When two identical vowels meet at a suffix boundary, one is dropped:

  • mach + se + e → machse (not machsee)
  • mach + se + en → machsen (not machseen)

Stress

Stress always falls on the first syllable of the root. Suffixes are never stressed — a useful aid for spotting root boundaries inside long agglutinated forms.

4. Nouns & Cases

Grammatical Gender

Every noun belongs to one of three genders. The gender is partly predictable from the ending, but as in German many nouns must be memorised. Articles agree with gender in the nominative.

GenderTypical endingsArticleExample
Masculine (m)-er, -en, bare consonantderder Hund (dog)

Articles are optional in Kesin — the case suffix is always unambiguous on its own. Use articles for emphasis or stylistic clarity.

Kesin uses eight cases, expressed by suffixes appended to the noun stem. The system combines the six classical Latin cases with the Turkish Locative and Instrumental:

Suffix Order

The suffix order on a noun is fixed: Stem → Plural (-ler) → Case suffix. Never reverse this order.

5. Articles & Determiners

Definite articles in the nominative:

  • der (m) · die (f) · das (n) · die (plural, all genders)

Indefinite articles in the nominative:

  • ein (m / n) · eine (f)

Because case is always marked on the noun itself, articles do not decline across cases in Kesin — only the noun suffix changes. This is a deliberate simplification over German, where both article and noun decline. Demonstratives (dieser, diese, dieses) follow the same pattern.

6. Pronouns

Personal pronouns are the only words in Kesin with their own irregular case forms (as in German and Latin). Reflexive Pronoun

The reflexive pronoun is sich, invariable across all persons. It replaces the object pronoun when subject and object coincide: Er sieht sich (He sees himself).

7. Adjectives

Agreement

Adjectives are invariable in the predicative position (after a linking verb). In the attributive position (before a noun) they take a simple suffix matching noun gender in the nominative:

  • -er (m): ein großer Hund — a big dog
  • -e (f): eine große Frau — a big woman
  • -es (n): ein großes Haus — a big house

Comparison

Comparatives add -er to the positive; superlatives add -st- (inflected for gender/case when used attributively).

PositiveComparativeSuperlativeEnglish
großgrößergrößt-big / bigger / biggest
gutbesserbest-good / better / best
schnellschnellerschnellst-fast / faster / fastest

To express "more … than", use … als …: Er ist größer als sie. (He is taller than she is.)

Notes on Special Tenses

  • Evidential Past (-miş-) — borrowed from Turkish. Use it when you did not personally witness the event: Er kommmişt = "He apparently came" (you were told this or inferred it from evidence).
  • Habitual / Aorist (-ar-) — marks regular habits and universal truths, distinct from the neutral Present Simple. Compare Ich mache Kaffee ("I'm making coffee, neutral") with Ich machare Kaffee ("I make coffee — as a habit").
  • Plusquamimperfect (-ibav-) vs Past Perfect Progressive (-avibiy-): both describe a past activity preceding another past event, but -ibav- stresses the state or habitual nature, while -avibiy- stresses the ongoing process.
  • Conditional (-würd-) — from German würde. Pair with the Conjunctive (-se-) for the protasis: Wenn ich Zeit hätte, ich machwürde — "If I had time, I would do it."

10. Moods & Voices

Conjunctive (Subjunctive) — -se-

The conjunctive replaces the tense marker entirely. It expresses hypothetical or subjunctive meaning and appears in conditional protases and polite constructions. Note the Double-Vowel Reduction rule: -se- + -e (1sg) → machse, not machsee.

Usage: Wenn ich Zeit machse — "If I were to make time / If I made time."

Optative — -ey-

The optative also replaces the tense marker. It expresses wishes, desires, and blessings ("may…", "I wish…"), inspired by the Latin/Greek optative.

  • Ich macheye. — May I do it / I wish I could do it.
  • Er macheyet. — May he do it.

Imperative

The imperative is formed independently of the slot system:

  • 2nd singular: bare root — Mach!
  • 2nd plural: root + -ler — Machler!
  • 1st plural ("let's"): use the normal 1pl form — Machen wir!

For 3rd person commands ("let him/her/them…"), use the Optative.

12. Word Order

Case marking makes Kesin largely word-order-free. Every noun argument is unambiguously marked, so constituents can appear in nearly any order.

OrderDescriptionExample
Default — SOVSubject → Object → Verb. Neutral statement.Ich den Hundi sehe.
Optional V2Verb shifts to second position (German influence). Stylistically marked.Ich sehe den Hundi.
Fronted topicAny constituent can be placed first for emphasis; verb follows in V2.Den Hundi sehe ich.
Free orderBecause case marks meaning, any order is possible.Sehe ich den Hundi.

Adverbs and adverbial phrases may appear almost anywhere. Modifying adjectives appear before the noun. Postpositions (from Turkish influence) follow their noun phrase.

14h · Numbers, Prepositions & Conjunctions

Numbers: null, eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn, elf, zwölf, zwanzig, hundert, tausend.

Prepositions: in, auf, an, mit (dat.), von (abl.), zu (dat.), für (acc.), ohne (acc.), durch (acc.), über, unter, zwischen, nach, vor, hinter, neben.

Conjunctions: und (and), oder (or), aber (but), weil (because — triggers verb-final order), obwohl (although), wenn (when / if — used in conditionals with -würd-), dass (that), damit (so that).

16. Beginner Lessons

Lesson 1 · Greeting & saying who you are

New material: personal pronouns ich, du; the verb sein (to be); the question marker -mi-.

Key phrases:

  • Ich seie Stefan. — I am Stefan.
  • Du seist Maria. — You are Maria.
  • Seimist du Maria? — Are you Maria?
  • Ja, ich seie Maria. / Nein, ich seimee.
Lesson 2 · Simple statements with Subject-Object-Verb

New material: accusative -i; verbs sehen, haben, machen.

Key phrases:

  • Ich den Hundi sehe. — I see the dog.
  • Du das Buchi habst. — You have the book.
  • Wir Broti machen. — We make bread.
Lesson 3 · Negation and yes/no questions

New material: negation -me-; question marker -mi- stacked with tense.

Key phrases:

  • Ich Kaffeei machme. — I don't make coffee.
  • Du Kaffeei machmist? — Are you making coffee?
  • Sie Brot machemist? — Doesn't she make bread?
Lesson 4 · Talking about the past and the future

New material: Simple Past -ib-; Future I -ecek-; Present Perfect -av-.

Key phrases:

  • Ich gestern Hundi sehibe. — I saw the dog yesterday.
  • Du morgen Brot machecekst. — You will make bread tomorrow.
  • Wir das Buchi schon lesaven. — We have already read the book.
Lesson 5 · Locations, possession & "with"

New material: Locative -da, Genitive -in, Instrumental -ile.

Key phrases:

  • Ich am Hausda seie. — I am at the house.
  • Das Buchi des Studentin liegt am Tischda. — The student's book lies on the table.
  • Ich mit meinem Freundile spreche. — I speak with my friend.
